# On Academic Writing in the Age of LLMs
by _Nedjma Ousidhoum_

Although there are plenty of articles offering advice on how to write good research papers (many of them better than anything I could write), I wanted to add my perspective as: i) someone who genuinely enjoys reading and writing papers and believes in their collective contribution to scientific progress; ii) a supervisor and educator; iii) a reviewer; and iv) a (senior) area chair who tries to make informed acceptance and rejection decisions.

I find myself repeating some of the same advice to the students I supervise and to people who ask me for feedback on their manuscripts. So, I thought I would share a few lessons I have learned over the years with people who genuinely want to do good science and write good papers but are inexperienced, non-native English speakers (I am one myself), or simply self-conscious about their writing. I am thinking, in particular, of those who might relate to my younger self, back when I used to sprinkle my writing with "fancy" connectors in an attempt to sound more eloquent, only for my MSc supervisor (now a friend) to ask me, in my mother tongue: "**Yikes**, why are you doing this?" 🙂

One might think that LLMs and other AI tools have made academic writing easier, particularly for non-native English speakers. In some respects, they have: grammar correction is easier, and tasks such as designing plots can now be done more quickly. The silver lining is that LLMs have helped demystify the "native speaker myth" in scientific writing, just as they have made it easier to write code for routine tasks and, in a way, brought the focus back to problem-solving, which is what computer science is supposed to be about. However, scientific writing has never been just about good English (or any language, for that matter). Nor has it been about impressive numbers or leaderboard positions alone.

I increasingly feel that academic narratives are becoming flattened, and that we are starting to see the same patterns, expressions and, occasionally, the same slop repeated over and over. Before sharing any advice, I want to acknowledge that writing is hard work (not a "soft" skill, whatever that means) and can be **[very]** frustrating and boring. However, I see our over-reliance on LLMs and AI tools as somewhat similar to the way societies have built cities around cars rather than people and nature, only to spend decades trying to convince people to cycle and walk more, go to the gym to prevent muscle atrophy, and take supplements. We should be careful not to make the same mistake with writing (and thinking). We need to recognise the threats early on, before we risk losing the patience and satisfaction that come from writing something from scratch.

## Advice to Newcomers (to NLP _and Adjacent Fields_)

### 1. Before writing, a note about reading: read full papers.

Getting better at writing takes practice and lots of reading. If you are a beginner, do not rely on summaries; read papers in their entirety. Reading full papers is valuable, and you do not need to understand everything to benefit from the experience. You gradually develop a taste for both research and good writing, pick up useful practices without necessarily realising it, and learn how other researchers build an argument. Reading more generally also helps you become a better scientist.

Overclaims and bad writing have always existed, but their sheer volume makes them all the more noticeable. Learning to recognise overclaims is also part of learning to read critically and, ultimately, to write responsibly.

### 2. Start with a bad draft, and start early.

We all write bad first drafts. (I write very bad first drafts.)

Start writing early! Start with your notes, even if they do not connect, and try to build an argument out of them. Move things around, identify gaps, and gradually develop a clear narrative (I will discuss some tips below).

There is value in struggling with your own ideas and figuring out how to connect them. Do not outsource that process before you have had a chance to develop it yourself. My suggestion is to try doing this without an LLM first, even if you eventually use one for feedback, editing, or grammar checks.

### 3. Say what your paper is about, not what it is not about.

There is a tendency to describe a contribution by first explaining what the paper is **not** about, only to eventually get to what it actually does. While this can be useful to a certain extent, for instance, when addressing common misconceptions, clarifying the scope of a paper, or discussing limitations, it should not become the default way of describing your contribution.

Lead with what you actually did. Be direct, precise, and concise. For example, say _"We investigate A through three experiments: a, b, and c"_ rather than framing your contribution as _"not only do we do a, but we also do b and c"_. Avoid unnecessary qualifications and elaborate constructions that delay getting to the point. Tell readers what you did, rather than making them work out your contribution from what you did not do.

Developing this skill takes practice, and it is harder than it sounds. It is perfectly fine to write something like this in an early draft, but make sure you edit it until the problem disappears from the final description of your contributions.

### 4. Think of your paper as a zoom lens.

Students I supervise may be tired of hearing my metaphor about **starting with a bird's-eye view and then gradually zooming in**. I also keep telling them that _"your conclusion is your introduction, but written for someone who has now read your paper"_. In other words, you can use terminology you have already introduced, refer to specific findings, and assume readers have followed your argument (as my MSc supervisor used to tell me). I have not come up with a better metaphor yet!

You do not need to have everything figured out before putting something on paper. When building your paper or dissertation, a useful exercise is to start with a set of questions: **Why** does the problem you are addressing matter? **What** exactly are you trying to address? **How** are you addressing it? (What do we already know? What don't we know? What are you trying to establish, and why should anyone care? What is the central message you want readers to take away?)

These questions can help you find the storyline before worrying too much about the actual prose. You will likely start with a set of disconnected sections. That is perfectly fine. Take the time to connect them gradually so that you give readers the bigger picture first and then progressively narrow the focus.

### 5. Writing an introduction is hard. Developing a storyline is very hard.

The introduction should take readers from the broader problem to the specific questions and contributions of your paper. The conclusion should take them back out again, connecting your findings to the bigger picture. The paper should ultimately tell a coherent story.

Sometimes there is that little something missing, but you cannot quite put your finger on it. It might be a logical gap, an unexplained assumption, or a connection between two ideas that you have not made explicit. Give yourself some time. Get some rest (or take a short break from that section if you are chasing a deadline), come back to the paper, try explaining your argument to someone else, or read it out loud. Sometimes the problem becomes obvious once you hear your own writing.

### 6. Do not vary your vocabulary just for the sake of avoiding repetition or sounding more like a native speaker.

There is nothing wrong with straightforward language! In fact, it is often much more enjoyable to read.

Write short, simple sentences and do not aim for "sophistication". It is perfectly fine to use "use" rather than "employ". You don't need to replace every familiar word with a supposedly fancier alternative. Repetition is not necessarily bad writing: sometimes using the same word is precisely what makes your writing clearer. In other words, do not sacrifice precision just to demonstrate your vocabulary; readers should not need a dictionary to understand NLP/ML papers. :-)

Also, although this is partly a matter of taste, I think you should own your contribution. Your paper or project does not "analyse", "investigate", or "present" something by itself. **You** did the work. You can say what "we" or "I" analyse, investigate, or present in the paper. There is nothing wrong with that.

### 7. Do not confuse fluent writing with meaningful content.

This is perhaps what others and I find increasingly concerning: empty content.

Good scientific writing is about meaningful communication. You can have perfectly grammatical sentences, rich vocabulary, and beautifully connected paragraphs that ultimately say very little (or nothing).

Every sentence should contribute something to the overall argument, and every paragraph should have a purpose. If you find yourself reading a paragraph and struggling to identify what it actually tells the reader, that might be a sign that you need to remove it or rethink it.

Writing is also about knowing what to remove. Sometimes the best edit is deleting a sentence, a paragraph, or even an entire section. Not everything you worked hard to write deserves to remain in the final paper. Do not feel bad about removing content; it served its purpose by helping you get to a better version of the paper.

### 8. Analysis is more than putting numbers and figures into words.

Your analysis should add value. It should not simply repeat what is already visible in a table or figure. Describe results, certainly, but do not mistake description for analysis.

Readers can see that a number is higher or lower, or that a particular method outperforms another. Your job is to help them understand what those results mean or think through intriguing observations, even if you do not have a straightforward answer. This can make your work even more interesting, as it opens the door to additional research questions. For instance: Why do we observe this pattern? Is it expected? What does it tell us? How does it relate to your research questions and previous findings?

Note that you do not always need to have an explanation for every unexpected observation. Sometimes acknowledging an interesting pattern, discussing possible explanations, and identifying questions for future work is more valuable than forcing an explanation that your evidence cannot support.

### 9. Back up your claims with evidence, no matter how convinced you are.

This might sound obvious, but it is worth emphasising in the age of multidisciplinary research: always provide evidence to support your claims, no matter how convinced you are of your position. Make sure your references actually support the claims you are making, and that you are drawing on relevant evidence rather than simply citing work because it is widely cited in the area. This becomes particularly important when making claims about topics that extend beyond your direct area of expertise, or when you hope your work will have an impact on other fields or communities. Be careful not to assume that your technical findings automatically translate into broader societal benefits, or that you fully understand the needs, priorities, and challenges of people working in other domains.

If you want to make claims beyond your immediate technical contribution, engage with the relevant literature and, where appropriate, with people who have expertise in those areas. Explain the reasoning and mechanisms connecting your contribution to the outcomes you anticipate, and be honest about what you have and have not established, even when you feel strongly about the topic and genuinely wish to contribute. You can discuss potential implications and broader ambitions, but distinguish them from demonstrated findings.

## Finally …

None of this is meant to suggest that there is only one way to write a good paper. We all still make mistakes! These are simply some of the things I have learned, some of the mistakes I have made, and some of the things I keep noticing when reading and reviewing papers.

Writing is a skill, and like any other skill, it requires practice, reading, feedback, and a fair amount of patience. But it is also rewarding: it helps you clarify your ideas, figure out what you actually want to say, learn how to construct an argument, and communicate it effectively.

Develop your own message and voice first, and then, if needed, use LLMs to assist you, not to replace you.
